The three-minute version

Cosmo

What it is, why it works the way it does, and what you need to try it. Seven questions, short answers.

So what is Cosmo?

It's a set of written guides (we call them skills), helper agents and a shared library of ready-made charts and slides, for AI assistants like Claude Code or Cursor.

You ask for a report, a deck or a dashboard in plain words. It comes out looking like everyone else's, and what the team learns along the way gets kept for next time.

Why build from a library instead of having the AI draw everything from scratch?

Because most of a report page is the same every time, and there's no point paying the assistant to rewrite it.

In one sample five-chart report, about 63% of the page was styling and chart code that never changes. The assistant itself wrote under about 1,000 tokens (a token is a chunk of text you pay for and wait on). That's one sample, not an average.

  • Every report has the same look.
  • New data means a rebuild, not a rewrite.
  • Less text written means less to pay for and less to wait for.

Why do we use a script to make presentations?

Because a script does the fiddly parts the same way every time.

It copies a slide from the library, fills its chart from your data, and checks that nothing spills off the slide. Then you edit a few lines of wording.

  • Every deck looks the same.
  • When the numbers change, you refresh the deck instead of rebuilding it.
  • A mistake shows up in a check, not in the meeting.
How it works, step by step
  1. The assistant picks a slide from the library by its id.
  2. The script copies it into your deck and points its chart at your columns.
  3. It checks every slide for text or charts that overflow, and takes screenshots.
  4. You, or the assistant, edit the wording on the slide.

What if the chart we want isn't quite in the library?

The closest one still goes in, with a note saying exactly what to change by hand. And when you end up with a chart you like, it gets saved back, so next time it's just there.

Two helpers handle this. One suggests which library items fit your request and says plainly what the library doesn't have. The other saves new visuals back into the library.

  • You never start from a blank page.
  • The library grows out of real work.

Why does the assistant need a brain?

Because it starts every conversation knowing nothing about your team, your data or what you decided last week.

The brain is a folder of short notes it reads first, and it only reads the few that matter for what you asked. New facts get filed after the work, but only when you say yes.

  • You stop explaining the same background every time.
  • It reads a handful of notes, not the whole folder.
  • What one person learns, the whole team can use.

Want the details? How the brain works

Why hand work to helpers instead of one long chat?

Because a long chat re-reads everything at every step, so each step costs more than the one before.

Instead, one planner keeps the thread, and short helper chats on cheaper models do the building. In one worked example that came to roughly $1.00 against $2.20 for the same job in a single chat. That's an estimate, not a measured bill.

  • Cheaper in the example we worked through.
  • Each helper sees only what it needs, so it stays on task.
  • The planner checks every result before it's used.

What do I need to start?

Not much. A setup page is on the way; for now, this is the list.

  • Claude Code or Cursor, signed in.
  • The Cosmo folder, copied into your assistant's settings folder.
  • Python and Node.js. The skills that build reports, decks and dashboards use them.
  • Edge or Chrome, so the assistant can look at what it made.
  • A folder of notes for the brain. Optional at first.
