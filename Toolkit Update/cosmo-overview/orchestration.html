Orchestration

How one planner and a few helpers get a big job done.

Why not just do everything in one long chat?

For a small job, do. For a big one, the chat fills up its context window, which is how much it can hold in mind at once. Every new step makes it re-read the whole conversation, so it gets slower and costs more. And what you decided at the start gets buried.

So what's the idea?

You talk to one planner, the orchestrator. It splits the job up and hands each piece to a helper, a subagent, that starts with a fresh memory. The helper does the work and sends back a short report. The planner only ever reads the reports.

  • The chat you're in stays short and focused.
  • Pieces that don't overlap run at the same time.
  • Each helper can use the model that suits its job.

What does that look like on a real question?

Say you ask: "How has claims trend changed month over month, and is there a notable difference in the number of high-cost claimants?" Here's the run.

  1. The planner settles the definitions. Which date counts, paid or service? What counts as "high-cost"? Then it agrees a short plan with you.
  2. A small model looks at the data once. It reports the columns, the date range and the row count.
  3. Two builders work at the same time. One does the month-over-month trend, the other counts high-cost claimants. Each adds a chart from the design library.
  4. A checker re-runs both. It confirms the numbers in the text match the data.
  5. The planner writes the answer from the four short reports.

Why give helpers different models?

A model is the AI engine behind an assistant. Bigger ones think better but are slower and dearer, so using one for every little job is wasteful. We match the model to the work.

Small, fast

A quick look

Reading the data once and reporting what's in it.

Mid-sized

Building and checking

The charts, the counts and the re-run.

Largest

Planning and judgement

Settling definitions and writing the answer.

Doesn't all that cost more?

Not for this one. As a rough estimate from assumed step counts, it comes to about $1.00 orchestrated, against about $2.20 in one chat on the largest model. It also finishes sooner, because the two builders run together. That's an estimate, not a measurement.

  • Shorter chats get re-read less: about 1.2M tokens in total instead of 3.2M. A token is roughly a word.
  • The building is done on a model that costs half as much.
  • The first look at the data is done on one that costs a fortieth as much.

One chat

Largest model only

About 40 steps. The chat grows from about 15,000 to about 150,000 tokens, so each step re-reads about 80,000 on average.

  • Re-reading: 40 × 80,000 = 3.2M tokens × $0.20 = $0.64
  • New material read (data peeks, results, errors): 135,000 × $5.00 = $0.68
  • Written (code, reasoning, the report): 45,000 × $20 = $0.90
  • Total: about $2.20

Largest plans, smaller build

  • Planner, largest model, about 12 steps at about 25,000 tokens: re-reading 0.3M × $0.20 = $0.06; new 20,000 × $5.00 = $0.10; written 8,000 × $20 = $0.16. About $0.32
  • Reader, small model: 60,000 read, 3,000 written. About $0.01
  • Builder A, mid-sized, about 15 steps at about 30,000: re-reading 0.45M × $0.10 = $0.05; new 40,000 × $2.50 = $0.10; written 15,000 × $10 = $0.15. About $0.30
  • Builder B, mid-sized: the same. About $0.30
  • Checker, mid-sized: about $0.10
  • Total: about $1.00

The honest part

It doesn't remove work, it moves it to shorter chats and cheaper models. For a question one chart can answer, one chat is cheaper. And the planner settles what "high-cost" means before anyone builds, because a small model shouldn't be the one deciding that.

What keeps it from going off the rails?

You stay in charge. The work also happens on a branch, a separate copy of the project's history, until you accept it. And none of this depends on one vendor's assistant.

  • Nothing gets built until you say yes to a short plan.
  • Each helper is told which files it may touch.
  • Automatic fix rounds have a fixed limit, so it can't loop.
